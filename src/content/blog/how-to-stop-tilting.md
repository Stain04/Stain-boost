<p>Most players don't lose their rank in a single bad game. They lose it in a <strong>session</strong>: one frustrating loss, then a rushed queue, a reckless play, another loss, an argument in chat, and three hours later they're a division lower than when they started. That spiral is tilt, and it costs more LP than any matchup, champion or teammate.</p>
<p>The good news is that tilt is predictable. It has clear warning signs, and a few simple rules stop it before it does real damage. This guide covers both.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-tilt">What Tilt Actually Is</h2>
<p>Tilt is when frustration starts making your decisions for you. You still feel like you're playing normally, but you take fights you'd normally avoid, you chase kills to "make up" for a death, you stop warding, and you blame teammates instead of looking at the map. The mechanics are the same; the <strong>decision-making is worse</strong> — and in League, decisions win games.</p>

<h2 id="warning-signs">The Warning Signs</h2>
<p>Learn to notice these in yourself. Any one of them is a signal to slow down:</p>
<ul>
<li>You queue up again within seconds of a loss, without a break.</li>
<li>You're typing to a teammate during the game — or thinking about what you'd like to type.</li>
<li>You make a risky play and think "it doesn't matter, this game is lost anyway".</li>
<li>You're playing to prove a point, not to win: fighting the laner who killed you instead of playing the map.</li>
<li>You're checking your LP after every game more than you're thinking about the game itself.</li>
<li>You're tired, hungry or it's late — and you "just want one more win" before stopping.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="rules">5 Rules That Stop Tilt Before It Costs LP</h2>

<div class="tip-card">
<div class="tip-number">Rule 01</div>
<h3>The Two-Loss Rule</h3>
<p>Two losses in a row means you stop ranked for the session. Play something else, take a walk, come back tomorrow. This one rule prevents most long losing streaks, because the third and fourth losses usually come from the frustration of the first two — not from your actual skill.</p>
</div>

<div class="tip-card">
<div class="tip-number">Rule 02</div>
<h3>Mute Chat Before the Game Starts</h3>
<p>Type <code>/mute all</code> during loading. You'll still have pings for everything important, and you remove the most common trigger for tilt: someone else's opinion of your play. Nobody has ever won a game by winning an argument in chat.</p>
</div>

<div class="tip-card">
<div class="tip-number">Rule 03</div>
<h3>Take a Short Break Between Every Game</h3>
<p>Stand up, drink water, look away from the screen for a few minutes. Starting each game fresh instead of carrying the last one into it makes a real difference to your focus in the first ten minutes — which is where lanes are won or lost.</p>
</div>

<div class="tip-card">
<div class="tip-number">Rule 04</div>
<h3>Set Goals You Control</h3>
<p>"Win this game" depends on nine other people. "Die fewer than 5 times", "ward the river before every dragon" and "crash the wave before every recall" depend only on you. Judge each game by your goal, not the result, and you'll stop feeling like your climb is out of your hands — and, over enough games, the results follow.</p>
</div>

<div class="tip-card">
<div class="tip-number">Rule 05</div>
<h3>Never Queue to "Win It Back"</h3>
<p>If you notice yourself queuing to recover LP you just lost, stop. That's the exact mindset that turns one loss into five. Your LP will still be there tomorrow, and you'll play better for it.</p>
</div>

<h2 id="in-game">Staying Calm During a Game</h2>

<h3>When You Die Early</h3>
<p>A first-blood death feels huge, but at this point the game is barely decided. Don't try to win it back in the next trade. Play safe for a few waves, farm, and let your item spikes and your jungler bring you back into it.</p>

<h3>When a Teammate Is Struggling</h3>
<p>Play around the lanes that are winning instead of trying to "fix" the one that isn't. Help the struggling player only when it's cheap — and never by typing at them. A tilted teammate plays worse; a muted, ignored one sometimes recovers.</p>

<h3>When the Game Looks Lost</h3>
<p>Plenty of games are thrown by the team that's ahead. Keep farming, keep your waves pushed, ward around Baron and wait for the enemy to make a mistake. The <a href="/blog/how-to-stop-throwing">guide to closing out games</a> lists the mistakes winning teams make — they're the ones you're waiting for.</p>

<h2 id="losing-streaks">Losing Streaks and LP</h2>
<p>Long losing streaks also hurt in a way you can't see: your hidden MMR drops, so the next wins can give less LP than the losses took. That's another reason to stop early — every avoided loss protects your MMR as well as your LP. If your LP gains already feel low, <a href="/blog/why-lp-gains-are-low">this guide to low LP gains</a> explains why and how to fix it.</p>

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>Review losses calmly — the next day.</strong> After a loss, write down the one moment where <em>you</em> could have made a better choice. Not your jungler, not your support: you. Reviewing it a day later, without the emotion of the game, turns losses into lessons instead of tilt.</p>
</div>

<h2 id="routine">A Simple Ranked Routine</h2>
<ol>
<li>Warm up for 5–10 minutes: practice tool last-hitting or one normal game.</li>
<li>Pick one goal you control for the session.</li>
<li>Mute all at loading; ping instead of typing.</li>
<li>Short break after every game, win or lose.</li>
<li>Stop after two losses in a row — or when you're tired.</li>
</ol>
<p>It sounds simple because it is. Consistency, not talent, is what keeps a climb going.</p>
